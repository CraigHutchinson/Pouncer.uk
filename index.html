Nature lives next door

A little more room for nature.

For the familiar faces in your garden, and the little lives you rarely see.

One British spring and summer, seen from the garden

A survey of nearly a thousand cats, scaled to the national population, put the number of prey items brought home between April and August at around 92 million. Not a statistic about cats — a count of individual animals that lived in gardens like yours.

Mammals are most of it

Songbirds get the attention, but small mammals are the larger share. Voles, shrews and wood mice are native wildlife, not vermin — so Pouncer is built for the low, flat pounce as much as the strike upward at a bird.

"Brought home" is the floor

These counts only include what a cat carried back through the door. Prey eaten or left outside is never counted, so the real figure is higher — nobody knows by how much.

Your cat is not the villain

The RSPB's position is that there is no clear evidence this mortality is driving UK bird populations down. We are not arguing from collapse. We are arguing for the individual animals in one garden, and for owners who would rather they lived.

An idea worth testing

A warning shaped around movement.

Your cat follows its instincts. Pouncer is being developed to recognise the subtle movement sequence around a pounce and use it to decide when to give a short warning cue.

The aim is selective sound, with ordinary activity left quiet. Whether that gives wildlife a useful chance to react is what testing must establish.

A calmer approach

Quiet by design.

Observe. Recognise the stalk. Confirm the launch. Then settle. Pouncer is being designed to read a sequence of movements, rather than react to every jump or playful tumble.

  1. 1

    Observe

    Continuous low-power sampling on the collar. Ordinary movement stays below the confidence threshold and produces no output at all.

  2. 2

    Stalk detected

    Sustained low-velocity stalking posture raises confidence. At this stage Pouncer can give a brief visual pulse only — no sound.

  3. 3

    Launch confirmed

    Launch acceleration on top of a confirmed stalk triggers one short, forward-directed chirp — the head start the whole design is aiming at.

  4. 4

    Cooldown & log

    Pouncer then stays acoustically quiet until motion settles and fresh evidence appears. Every candidate — alerted or suppressed — is logged for review.

Design intent, not a demonstrated outcome. The classifier is still in development. A three-axis accelerometer samples at 100 Hz. Ten-second windows: Each event stores five seconds either side of the trigger on the device, then offloads over Bluetooth LE to the companion app so you can see what your cat actually did.

Considered in every detail

A small module. A thoughtful design.

A single machined disc on the collar your cat already wears. It has no screen and nothing to read — the whole face is there to hold a lens, a sounder and a seal. Built to the tolerances of something worn every day, outdoors, by an animal who will not be careful with it.

What we will commit to now

  • Fits a standard quick-release collar — you keep your strap and buckle
  • A user-replaceable cell, not a sealed unit thrown away at first failure
  • No GPS and no location tracking — it records motion, not where your cat is
  • Events download to your own device; no cloud account to use the product

What we will not print yet

  • Weight, dimensions, battery life, water rating
  • Price

Those are measurements, and we do not have them. They get published when the hardware they describe exists — and not one release earlier.

Predictive motion engine

Built to track micro-movements and recognise the acceleration signature of a launch as it begins, rather than report an impact after the fact.

Silent until it counts

No chiming while your cat eats, sleeps or walks. Hysteresis, dwell times and rate limits are specified specifically to keep nuisance alerts near zero — and the collar can be worn with alerts off entirely.

Built to keep, not to replace

A serviceable module rather than a sealed one, on the collar you already own — so buying Pouncer does not mean throwing working equipment away.

What the bell evidence tells us

The table reports one trial, not the whole literature. Bell results are mixed: some studies found fewer prey brought home, while the 2021 trial above found no discernible effect. See the wider evidence.

A mechanical bell responds to movement; it does not classify behaviour. Pouncer is being developed to recognise a stalk-and-launch sequence and trigger a brief cue. That difference is a design hypothesis, not evidence of better timing or fewer animals caught.

Pouncer's detection timing, false alerts, cat welfare and wildlife outcomes all need testing.

Where Pouncer actually is

We would rather be trusted than hyped. Pouncer is a working engineering programme, not a finished product on a shelf, and these are the honest boundaries.

  • Built

    Inertial sampling, on-device event storage and Bluetooth LE offload to a companion application are the current development focus.

  • In progress

    Classifier tuning against real recorded cat motion — birds and small mammals — and the directional acoustic enclosure: forward output, attenuated toward the cat.

  • In progress

    Warning lead time. Measuring how long a real pounce lasts and how fast the collar can decide — because a cue that lands with the strike has warned nobody. If we cannot beat the clock, the device logs the event and makes no sound.

  • In progress

    The prey-outcome trial. Alerts-on versus alerts-off on the same cat, results broken out by prey type, published either way.

  • On the roadmap

    Learning your individual cat. Individual calibration is a future research goal. The event log is being designed to support it, but any improvement in detection remains to be measured.

  • On the roadmap

    More platforms. Windows desktop is first and Android is next, with iOS and macOS after them. The portable core carries no platform toolkit, so each one is a new front end rather than a rewrite — which is why they are on the list at all, and why none of them has a date.

  • On the roadmap

    Finding a lost collar, and the cat wearing it, by letting the module advertise as a Bluetooth LE beacon. Garden-sized range, not street-sized, and not Find My. Optional, and only if the broadcast can be made unrecognisable to anyone but your own phone.

  • Not yet claimed

    Any reduction in prey caught. Final alert frequency and level. Battery life. Those numbers get published when measurement supports them — not before.

Pouncer is designed to give wildlife a warning, not to guarantee an outcome. It is a complement to diet, play and supervised outdoor time, not a substitute for them, and nothing here is veterinary advice.

Questions

Will the alert distress my cat?

That is the central design constraint. The chirp is short, forward-directed and mechanically attenuated toward the cat's ears, and it fires rarely by design. Welfare testing gates the final acoustic profile — if a profile bothers cats, it does not ship.

I don't mind my cat catching mice. Isn't that pest control?

Mostly it isn't. The mammals cats bring home in Britain are overwhelmingly native species — bank voles, field voles, shrews and wood mice — rather than the commensal brown rats and house mice people picture as pests. Shrews in particular are protected under UK law and are frequently caught and left uneaten. Pouncer also cannot tell one small mammal from another: it is built to detect the pounce, not the prey.

How is Pouncer different from a bell?

Pouncer is being developed to recognise motion patterns and choose when to give a cue. A bell is a mechanical sound-maker, with mixed results across wildlife studies. We have not demonstrated that Pouncer warns earlier or reduces catches more effectively. Read the evidence and the limits.

Will it learn my cat's habits?

Individual calibration is a future goal, not a current feature. The first classifier is being developed from recorded motion. The event log is being designed to support later work on individual differences, but we have not demonstrated faster or more accurate detection from personalisation.

What stops it firing during play?

A single hard acceleration is never enough. The classifier requires a stalk phase, then launch features, then a confirmation window — followed by a cooldown that needs fresh evidence before it can sound again.

Shouldn't I just keep my cat indoors?

That is the most effective option, and for some cats and some locations it is the right one. It is also not a choice every owner can or will make, and a cat with outdoor access and no intervention is the status quo Pouncer is trying to improve on. We would rather help the owner who is staying outdoors than lecture them.

Does it replace changing food or playing with my cat?

No — and those have better evidence behind them than anything we have proven yet. A high-meat-content food and ten minutes of daily play both reduced prey brought home in a controlled trial. Do those first. Pouncer is designed to work alongside them.

Does it track my cat's location?

No. Pouncer records motion events, not position, and there is no GPS in the module. Events stay on the collar until you download them over Bluetooth LE.

The beacon below does not change that. A beacon broadcasts "I am here" to anything listening nearby; it never learns where it is, and nothing about your cat's position is computed, stored or sent anywhere. Your phone estimates how close you are from how strong the signal is. That is the whole mechanism.

If the collar comes off, can I find it? Can I find the cat?

That is on the roadmap, and it is not in the first release. The module can advertise as a Bluetooth LE beacon, so a phone in range can tell you whether you are getting warmer or colder. It is a hotter-and-colder game, not a pin on a map.

Be clear about the limits, because this is the feature people most want to over-read. The range is short — think the length of a garden rather than the length of a street — and a hedge, a wall, a shed door or the cat's own body all cut it down further. We will not print a range figure until we have measured one. It is good for a collar that came off in the long grass, and for a cat that is somewhere in the house refusing to answer. It is no help at all for a cat two streets away.

It is also not Apple's Find My, and not Google's Find My Device. Those are separate accessory programmes with their own cryptography and certification, and being a Bluetooth beacon does not put a device into either of them. If that ever changes we will say so; until then, assume the only phone that can hear your collar is one you are holding.

And a beacon that shouts a fixed identifier all day is a way to follow the person carrying it. So: it will be optional, and we will not ship one whose broadcast can be recognised by anyone except the phone it is paired with. If we cannot do that, we will ship no beacon at all.

Which phone or computer do I need? Is there an iPhone app?

The first companion is Windows desktop, with Android next. iOS and macOS are on the roadmap — later than the other two, but on it. iPhone owners are not being designed out; they are being asked to wait, and we would rather say that plainly than leave it unsaid.

The reason is that one platform shipped properly beats four that half work. The portable core — the event format, the protocol, the pounce analysis — is deliberately written without any platform toolkit in it, so adding a platform is a new front end rather than a rewrite. That is a design decision made now, for a version that comes later. It is not a release date, and we will not give one until it is real.

Does it work with my existing collar?

It is designed as a compact module for standard quick-release collars, so the break-away safety mechanism you already use stays in place.
